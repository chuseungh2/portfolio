01

Context & problem

Most startups begin as a single good idea. The founder is usually a designer, an engineer, or a domain expert — rarely a business operator. Yet the moment the idea becomes a company, the business side arrives all at once: operations, scheduling, planning, coordination, admin. It's work that has to happen, and it quietly eats the hours that should go into the product itself.

There are AI tools that promise to help. But they tend to be fragmented — a different tool for every task — and most assume the user is already comfortable with AI agents, prompts, and configuration. For a founder who just wants their business to run, that's one more thing to learn, not a thing that helps.

Asah started as a project for the Amazon Nova Hackathon. That original version was built for first-time founders specifically: it scored a business idea for viability — strengths, risks, revenue projections — scanned uploaded contracts and legal documents for risk, and laid out a roadmap of what to do next. It didn't win. But the idea resonated enough — with us, if not with the judges — that the team decided not to leave it as a hackathon entry, and rebuilt it into the web-first, agent-based product described below.

Who it's for: solo founders and small startup teams who don't have business expertise on the team — and who aren't familiar with AI agents either.

Constraints: a hackathon timeline to start, a team of three, and a build on Amazon Nova.

This isn't a hypothetical gap: founders already pay for the pieces of it separately — outsourced ops help, scattered business-management SaaS, sometimes a part-time hire just to keep the admin from piling up. Asah's bet is that a single manager replaces the stack, not that it invents a need nobody has. [Kelly: if you have a real number or example from the competitor research in 03 — what founders typically spend on this today, or a specific tool people pay for — it'd land harder here than the general claim above.]

Three terms, if this space is new to you: an AI agent is software that can plan and carry out multi-step actions on its own, not just answer a single question. RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) is how a model pulls in real, specific information — in Asah's case, a business's own data — before it answers, instead of relying only on what it learned during training. Amazon Nova is Amazon's family of foundation models, the base Asah's agents run on.

02

My role

Backend (shared) Shared design Shared research

Team of three. Design and research were shared — all three of us shaped the concept, the users, and the direction together.

The backend and knowledge layer were also team work — I built it alongside a teammate, leaning on AI-assisted coding for both of us, and at this distance I can't cleanly separate which lines were whose. What I can speak to is the thinking: what the agent contract had to guarantee (an honest "uncertain" instead of a confident guess), and why the knowledge layer needed to ground an agent in the specific company it's working for, not answer in generalities. See 06 How it works for what that actually looks like in the code.

On the design side, the line I kept pushing every team decision back to: friendly enough that a first-time AI user isn't intimidated, but never so playful that it stops reading as a serious business tool. A business manager that looks childish loses trust the moment a founder considers handing real work to it. That tension — warm but credible — is the rule the whole interface was tuned against, backend included.

03

Research

We didn't have time for primary research with founders, so we ran a focused secondary-research pass to map the problem space and the competitive landscape before committing to a direction. We looked at:

  • What running a startup actually requires — the operational, financial, and managerial workload that isn't "the product."
  • Where founders consistently struggle — across reports, founder accounts, and post-mortems.
  • Why startups fail — failure-rate data and the most common reasons cited.
  • The existing tools — a competitor analysis of AI assistants and business-management tools aimed at small teams.

The findings that shaped the design:

  1. Most startups fail for reasons that have nothing to do with the idea itself. Operations, money, and team management show up far more often than a weak product. → That confirmed our focus: take the business side off the founder, not the product side. [Kelly: if there's a specific number or source you remember (e.g. "X% of startups fail because of…"), drop it in.]
  2. The founders who struggle most don't have a business operator on the team. Many small startups are technical or design-led, and the business workload lands on whoever has time. → That sharpened our target audience: solo founders and small teams without that expertise.
  3. The tools that exist are either fragmented or too technical. Most assume the user is already fluent in AI agents, prompts, and configuration. → That set the differentiation: not another tool to learn, but a single manager that handles the agents on the user's behalf. [Kelly: if there were 2-3 specific competitors you compared against, name them here — concrete names land harder than "existing tools."]
  4. Founders don't know what to do once they've actually launched. Idea-stage guidance is everywhere; reports and founder accounts kept surfacing the same gap: once a business is actually running, there's no clear signal for the next move. → This is the finding that outlasted the pivot itself: the hackathon version answered it with a static roadmap screen (see Decision 02); the current one answers it by having the agents plan against a founder's actual goals and surface what's next directly, instead of a checklist everyone walks through the same way.
04

Goal

Asah didn't have a metric to hit — no shipped product yet, no user base to measure. What it needed instead was a bar the concept itself had to clear, at two different points.

Right after the hackathon: was there a version of this worth building past the submission? The bar we set was two-part — a specific enough target user, and a real difference from the AI tools that already exist. Without both, continuing past the hackathon wasn't worth it.

For the product itself: could someone who has never touched an AI agent hire one, trust it, and rely on it — without seeing a configuration screen or writing a line of code? That became the test every design decision below got run against.

05 ★

Decisions & tradeoffs

01

From hackathon app to web product, with a sharper direction

The version we submitted to the hackathon was a mobile app. After submitting, we asked an honest question: if this were a real product, would it survive? The answer was no — our target audience wasn't specific enough, and the product didn't feel different enough from the AI tools already out there.

So we redrew the idea. Instead of "an app with AI features," Asah became an AI business manager — positioned like a real employee, not a tool. The user hires other AI agents as workers, and Asah supervises all of them, so the user never has to. That gave us both a clear audience (founders without business expertise) and a clear difference from everything else. We also moved to web-first: the work Asah handles fits a larger screen better than a phone. (A mobile version is still planned — but web is the home.)

The tradeoff: we walked away from a finished hackathon app and committed to a much larger build. We decided a clear, defensible product was worth more than a head start.

02

The roadmap became a behavior, not a screen

The hackathon version had an explicit roadmap: a checklist a founder could see and step through — idea validation, business planning, legal setup, MVP development, launch. It came from a real gap: founders kept saying that once a business was actually running, they didn't know what to do next (see Research). Idea-stage guidance is everywhere; after launch, it dries up.

That screen didn't survive the pivot, but the instinct behind it did. Instead of one fixed checklist every founder walks through the same way, the job moved into the manager itself: agents plan against a founder's actual goals and tell them what's next, tailored to where that specific business is — not a generic five-step list rendered the same for everyone.

The tradeoff: a checklist is scannable at a glance in a way a conversation isn't. We decided personalized was worth more than skimmable — the same call as Decision 04, just applied to planning instead of agent setup.

03

A manager you don't have to manage

The hardest part of "just use AI agents" is usually the user. Agents are powerful, but configuring them, prompting them, and keeping track of them is its own skill — and our users, by definition, don't have it.

So we made supervision Asah's job, not the user's. The user hires agents; Asah monitors and coordinates them. The interface is beginner-friendly on purpose: someone who has never touched an AI agent should be able to put quality agents to work without seeing a single line of configuration or code.

04

Agents as characters

If we described an AI agent the way it actually is — a prompt, a tool list, a configuration — most of our users wouldn't know what to do with it. They aren't AI users. They're founders.

So we treated each agent as a character: a name, a role, a personality, traits you can read at a glance. The thing under the hood is still an AI agent; the thing the user sees is closer to a teammate they can size up in two seconds. Characters carry meaning faster than prompts do — and for a product whose whole pitch is "you don't need to be technical to use this," that mattered more than realism.

06

How it works

The beginner-friendly interface in the sections above is the visible half of Decision 02. This is the half underneath it — the system that has to actually absorb the complexity for the UI to get away with hiding it. What's below describes the current web build, which has moved on from the version we submitted to the hackathon in Decision 01.

One contract, swappable engines. The app doesn't talk to agents directly — it talks to a single interface, AgentRuntime: send a request, get back an output with a status of ok, uncertain, or error, plus the sources an agent found its answer from. Whichever real backend is actually answering gets swapped in behind that one interface. Until an engine is plugged in, the default implementation returns an error instead of a made-up answer — the "don't guess" rule from Decision 02 isn't just house style here, it's the type the code compiles against.

Real backends, not mocks, for three agents. An Email agent (behind Google OAuth), a Research agent, and an SDR agent each run as their own Python (FastAPI) service, and each is reached through one file that owns every request and response mapping for that agent — so a backend change touches one seam, not the app. Getting an agent this far — a working auth flow, a real HTTP contract, a UI that survives a slow or failed response instead of just hanging — turned out to be most of the actual engineering, more than the chat surface itself.

What "uncertain" looks like in the product: the Research agent makes this literal. Every claim in a report carries a confidence label (high / medium / low / unverified) and stays collapsed behind its own citations until a user opens it — trustworthy enough to read at a glance, the receipts one click under it if you want them. That progressive-disclosure shape is "warm but credible" (Decision 02) built into a data schema, not just a chat tone.

A constraint that reshaped the interface: Research reports cost real money per request — several model calls per report — and can take anywhere from seconds to a couple of minutes to come back. That's why every request in the app is cancelable mid-flight instead of trapping the user behind a spinner they can't back out of. The backend's cost shaped a piece of the frontend contract.

Supervision, made visible: a running activity log records only real agent executions — never the seed/demo agents — each with what it did and a one-line result ("3 leads from 20 reviewed"), and a link back to where it happened. It's the closest thing on the page to watching the manager actually manage.

RAG — where it stands, honestly: the knowledge layer is being rebuilt right now — Postgres with pgvector, documents chunked and embedded, retrieved before an agent answers — and it isn't finished. An earlier version of Asah, the one built for the hackathon, had a working version of exactly this, built for a different job: scanning a founder's uploaded contracts and legal documents for risk. Documents were chunked at 1,000 characters with a 200-character overlap, embedded with Amazon Titan Text Embeddings V2, searched with FAISS, answered by Amazon Nova, and cited by source in the response — with a jurisdiction-rules database as a fallback so a search that came up empty still returned something grounded instead of nothing. That's the bar the current rebuild is aimed at, not a hypothetical.

What isn't built yet: the verification loop "Asah supervises agents" implies — one agent's output getting checked before a user sees it. The contract has room for it (that's what the uncertain status is for), but nothing assigns it automatically today. That gap is real, and it's what Reflection points at under "what's next."

One small detail from the design side, because it's mine directly: in the Research agent's report cards, every number inside a claim gets pulled out and set in the brand color, so scanning a paragraph gets you the scale of a finding without reading every sentence — and each claim carries a small confidence-colored dot ahead of its text, so a low-confidence claim is flagged before you've read it, not labeled after. Small, but it's the same "warm but credible" rule from Decision 02, applied to a data table instead of a chat bubble.

Stack: Vite + React + TypeScript on the frontend; Python (FastAPI) for the agent backends; storage moving from local-only to Supabase (Postgres with row-level security, plus pgvector for the RAG rebuild above).

08

Outcome

The hackathon didn't end in a prize. The signal that mattered came from the design itself: the beginner-friendly UI — the part we'd been least sure about — turned out to be the part that kept working. It's what told us Asah was worth continuing past the hackathon, and it's the thread the current web version is built around.

Asah isn't finished yet, so we haven't put it in front of users formally. But friends we showed it to along the way came back with the same three things, unprompted: it's well built, the idea is good, it's beginner-friendly. The last one is the one we'd been most uncertain about — the fact that it kept surfacing without us asking told us the design was carrying its weight.

09

Reflection

What went well. The pivot. Sitting with the project after the hackathon and being willing to say "the audience isn't clear enough, and we don't look different enough" took Asah from a hackathon entry to a real product direction. The beginner-friendly UI followed from that clarity, not the other way around.

What was hardest. Connecting the backend to the frontend. This was my first time doing backend at all — getting the API keys set up, building the RAG architecture from scratch, and getting Amazon Nova to actually return the right kind of response were all new to me, and the seams between systems were where the hard parts lived. I leaned on AI-assisted coding heavily, but the design of what to ask for, and the responsibility for whether the answer was right, was mine.

What I'd do differently. The Office space — where the user sees their hired agents — works, but it could feel more like an actual 2D office. I'd push the characters further: have them visibly doing the work, not just standing there. The metaphor we chose was the right one; I'd just commit to it harder.

What's next. Finishing the idea and finishing the backend, so Asah works end to end and not just on the design side. The web version is the home; the mobile version comes after.
