Today's Stock

She didn't need a new tool. She needed the one she already had to be set up for her.

The problem

My mom spends hours a day following the stock market through Korean YouTube, news, and Telegram digests. She knows AI could help. She'd tried, and ChatGPT handed the decision straight back to her, so she stopped.

The outcome

I set out to build her something. The research said not to. So instead of a product I designed the configuration: the rules, the shape of every answer, and the words it's allowed to use. It failed its first test with her, got rebuilt around that failure, went into a real purchase decision, and then had to solve the one thing she wanted that it legally couldn't say.

01

Context & problem

My mom is a Korean woman in her 50s who started learning about stocks to build financial independence. Every day she has time, she watches Korean stock YouTube channels, joins investment livestreams, and reads the news: hours of manual research, repeated daily, to assemble a picture that goes stale overnight.

She knows AI is powerful. But it read as something for a younger generation, not for her, so she stuck to what felt familiar. When she did try ChatGPT for stocks, it told her "investment decisions are yours to make," and she walked away with nothing. That wasn't the first time: she'd once asked it to find her the cheapest flight and gotten the same kind of answer: figure it out yourself. After that she stopped bringing it anything open-ended.

The thing is, she isn't someone who can't use AI. She already uses ChatGPT successfully at the doctor's office, asking it to help explain her symptoms in English. That works because the task is bounded and clear: one question, one answer, nothing to configure. Stock analysis is open-ended, and no one had ever set the tool up for that shape of task.

I started this project intending to build her a product. That intention is what the research took apart. See 03 Research and Decision 01.

Who it's for: my mom, specifically. Not a persona standing in for "users in their 50s."

Constraints: U.S. market news arrives in English, which is a wall for her. Under Korean financial law, an AI assistant cannot give unlicensed buy/sell/timing advice, full stop, regardless of how it's framed. And whatever I built had to cost $0 permanently and survive past the end of the project. [Kelly: class timeline or any other course constraints worth naming here]

One term, if this is unfamiliar: a Custom GPT is a configured version of ChatGPT: a system prompt, a few settings, and a shareable link. It runs inside the ChatGPT app the user already has. There is no separate app to install and no interface to design, which is exactly why it fit here.

02

My role

ResearchConversation designSystem promptTesting

Solo project. I did the research, the design argument, the system prompt across three versions, the conversation-starter copy, and three rounds of user testing.

The part I'd point to first: I'm also the subject's daughter. That meant I could sit with her, watch how she actually used it, and get her real reaction instead of a guess at what a "50-something user" might think. It also meant the stakes were personal in a way a hypothetical persona never is. She's the user I love most, and the one I wanted least to confuse or mislead.

03

Research

I didn't run a broad user study. This project had one user, and she's my mom. What I did was closer to a single deep contextual inquiry: interviewing her, then watching how she actually behaves with AI today, across more than one task, before designing anything for a new one.

What came back

  • She isn't someone who "can't use AI." Task complexity is what determines success, not the person. She uses ChatGPT successfully for bounded tasks, like translating her symptoms at the doctor's office. She struggled with anything open-ended: stocks, flights, general research. The variable wasn't her skill or her age. It was whether the task had a shape the tool could take.
  • The thing she was missing wasn't a tool. It was a setup. This is the finding that redirected the whole project. She already had ChatGPT, already had access through my dad's subscription, already had the habit. What she didn't have was a way to get what she wanted out of it without knowing how to write a good prompt, and "learn to write better prompts" was itself the barrier she'd already bounced off. So the deliverable stopped being a product and became a configuration.
  • What she wants most is exactly what the GPT is legally not allowed to give her. Her first pain point is buy/sell timing confusion. The first rule is no buy/sell recommendations, under any framing, under Korean financial law. That's the project's central design conflict, and the thing Decisions 02 and 03 are both trying to solve.
  • Chat, not voice: interview data beat my own assumption. My first draft of the platform rationale listed voice mode as an advantage for an older user. She told me directly: chat is more comfortable. She's never used voice mode. I dropped it from the plan entirely.
  • "Decide for yourself" isn't help, even when it's correct. What she actually wanted turned out to be two specific, answerable things: a read of the previous night's U.S. market in terms of today's Korean market, and the conditions under which a buy/sell boundary makes sense. Both are information, not advice. Both are inside what the GPT can legally give.

04

Goal

There was no metric to move here. One user, no analytics, and a tool that would either become part of her morning or quietly go unopened. So the bar had to be behavioral.

The test I held it to: could she get what she came for without knowing how to ask for it? Not "does the GPT answer correctly." ChatGPT already answered correctly, and she'd stopped using it anyway. The bar was whether a short, careless, half-formed question still came back with something she could act on. If she had to learn a technique to get value out of it, it had failed, however good the answers were.

The second bar came out of the legal constraint: a refusal had to leave her better off than before she asked. That one turned out to be the whole project.

05 ★

Decisions & tradeoffs

01

Don't build a product. Configure the one she already opens.

A thing to install → a setup for the thing she has

I came into this wanting to make something. The obvious options were all things: a bot on KakaoTalk, the messaging app she actually lives in, or a purpose-built assistant on whichever model handled financial reasoning best. There was a whole feature comparison arguing for Gemini or Claude over ChatGPT. There was also a version that pushed her a briefing every morning, and a version that was one all-in-one "Mom GPT" handling stocks, travel, and health together.

I rejected all of it, because the research had already answered the question and I'd been asking the wrong one. She wasn't short a tool. She had a capable one, open on her phone, that she was already using successfully for something else. What she lacked was a version of it configured for this job: one that gives her what she wants from a short, low-effort question, instead of demanding she first learn to ask well.

The rejections each had their own reason, and they all pointed the same way:

  • No new platform. A feature comparison optimizes for the wrong variable. Same app, same login, same interface, nothing new to learn, and $0 ongoing, because the subscription was already paid for.
  • No automatic push. Her stated pain point is that there's too much news. A daily push recreates the exact problem the thing exists to solve. It stays pull-based: she opens it when she wants a read.
  • No all-in-one assistant. In a stock GPT, "how's it looking today?" means market conditions; in a travel GPT it could mean weather. Her mental model shifts by task, so the tool has to shift by task.

The tradeoff, stated plainly: this decision cost me the thing a portfolio usually wants. There are no screens, no interface, no visual system to show. The design work lives in the configuration and the conversation, not in pixels. It was the right call for her and I'd make it again; it just means this case has to be read rather than looked at.

02

Refuse, but never send her away empty-handed

Soften the wording → rewrite the rule

The first version (v1.1) followed the rule correctly: no buy/sell recommendations. When my mom asked a trade question, it told her the decision was hers to make. She didn't ask a follow-up. Her feedback was blunt:

"I asked but it just told me to decide on my own?"

That's the identical pattern to why she'd given up on ChatGPT for stocks, and for finding a flight, in the first place. I had rebuilt her original failure and shipped it back to her.

The instinct, mine and the AI-assisted drafting's both, was to soften the wording. I rejected that. It wasn't a tone problem; it was structural. She left with nothing, twice, for the same underlying reason. So I rewrote the rule itself. The refusal is still one sentence, but the system prompt now makes it illegal for the answer to end there:

매매 관련 질문을 받으면, 거절만 하고 끝내지 마세요. 빈손으로 돌려보내지 않습니다. 사용자가 "물어봐도 소용없네"라고 느끼지 않게 합니다.

"When you get a trade question, don't just refuse and stop there." / "Never send her away empty-handed. Never let her feel that asking was pointless."

Every trade question now gets the one-sentence refusal followed immediately by the judgment material: the bullish case, the bearish case, the U.S.-market connection, the specific signals to watch, and condition-by-condition guidance. The rule stayed exactly as firm. What changed is that "no" was never the whole answer.

The same principle held at the level of a single word. An early conversation-starter draft read "What stocks are worth paying attention to right now?", which reads as a recommendation before she's typed anything. I replaced it with "What stocks are people talking about lately?" Same information, no implied advice. The boundary can't only live in the system prompt; it has to hold in the first sentence she reads.

03

Give her the price bands. Change the words, not the answer.

A verdict she can't have → the same numbers, framed as conditions

The second test went well, and she immediately asked for more: a price band for short-term spikes, and whether a short-term dip means hold or take profit. Both are the thing the GPT categorically cannot say. This is the central conflict from Research, arriving as a concrete feature request.

The easy readings were both bad. Give her the price bands and it's unlicensed investment advice, and she'd trust it, which is what makes the harm real rather than theoretical. Refuse again and I'm back to Decision 02's failure, telling her the thing she most wants is the one thing I won't discuss.

What I found instead was that the conflict wasn't in the information. It was in the vocabulary. A price level described as a buy price is a recommendation. The same price level described as a range where a particular signal gets confirmed is information. So I built the distinction into the system prompt as an explicit forbidden/permitted list:

Forbidden Permitted
홀딩하세요 · 차익실현하세요 "Hold it." · "Take your profits." 홀딩 쪽으로 볼 수 있는 조건 "the conditions under which holding is a reasonable read"
여기서 사세요 · 여기서 파세요 "Buy here." · "Sell here." 차익실현을 고민할 만한 조건 "the conditions that make taking profit worth considering"
매수가 / 매도가 "buy price / sell price" 과열 확인 구간 · 지지 확인 구간 · 위험 확인 구간 "overheating-confirmation range · support-confirmation range · risk-confirmation range"

She gets the numbers she asked for. They arrive as conditions to check rather than instructions to follow. The legal line holds, and she isn't sent away.

The tradeoff: this asks more of her than a straight answer would. "Support-confirmation range" is a concept she has to hold in her head, where "buy at this price" would not be. I decided that was the right trade. She's studying the market daily and building real expertise, so the version of this tool that respects that is one that hands her better material, not one that hands her a verdict she'd have to take on faith.

It held. When she tested this version, the improvement she singled out was the supply-and-demand read, the exact material the new vocabulary made sayable. See 07 Outcome.

06

The design

There are no screens in this project. The interface is ChatGPT's, unchanged. What I designed is what happens inside it: the rules, the shape of every answer, the words it can use, and the four sentences she can tap instead of writing a prompt.

The rules

The system prompt opens with the absolute rule, no buy/sell/timing calls and no predicting a price under any framing, and then spends most of its length on what the GPT must do instead of refusing. Two more that matter:

  • Never invent. If it can't verify something, it says so plainly rather than guessing a number. For a user who would believe it, that isn't a nice-to-have.
  • Say the price might be stale. Prices come from web search, so every answer carrying one notes that it's from a moment ago, once, without nagging.

Her holdings sit directly in the prompt as plain text rather than as an uploaded knowledge file. A knowledge file uses search-based retrieval, which is overkill for a short list and adds a way for the answer to be wrong. Simple-when-simple-is-correct is a design call too.

The shape of an answer

Every judgment question gets the same five beats, in this order:

  1. One-line conclusion. The character of the day ("a day to watch carefully"), never "buy" or "sell"
  2. Two or three reasons to see it positively
  3. Two or three reasons for concern
  4. The boundaries to check. U.S. market flow, foreign investor flow, exchange rate, earnings or major news, whether it's recently spiked or corrected
  5. Condition-by-condition hints. Good signals stacking → interest; signals conflicting → wait; bad signals growing → caution

I rejected the standard analyst report format (background → current situation → outlook) to get here. Report structure is how analysts write for other analysts. She needs a read, not a report. The conclusion goes first, and the reasoning sits underneath it for her to check rather than wade through. Default length is capped at three to five lines, extended only when she asks for more.

The first thing she sees

The greeting sets the boundary before she can bump into it:

안녕하세요! 주식 뉴스나 어려운 말을 쉽게 정리해 드리는 도우미예요. 편하게 물어봐 주세요. 다만 사고파는 결정은 직접 하셔야 해요. 저는 판단에 도움이 되는 정보만 드려요.

"Hello! I'm here to make stock news and difficult words easy to follow. Ask me anything. Just remember, buying and selling decisions have to be yours; I only give you what helps you judge."

Once per conversation, not repeated. The rule arrives as a friendly aside rather than as a wall she hits after asking.

The conversation starters

The part that does the most work for the least visible effort: four questions sitting there as buttons, taken from what she actually asked me during research:

어젯밤 미국 시장 보고 오늘 한국 주식시장 방향 알려줘 오늘 내 종목들 좋은 뉴스, 나쁜 뉴스로 나눠서 정리해줘 [종목] 단기 급등이면 가격밴드와 수급 기준 알려줘 내 종목 단기 하락이면 홀딩/차익실현 판단 기준 알려줘

"Look at last night's U.S. market and tell me the direction for Korean stocks today" · "Sort today's news on my holdings into good and bad" · "If [holding] has spiked short term, tell me the price bands and the supply-demand criteria" · "If my holdings dip short term, tell me the criteria for holding vs. taking profit"

She doesn't have to know how to write a prompt, because the prompts are already written, and they're hers. Prompt engineering, moved off the user and into the setup. That's the whole thesis in one interface element. [Kelly: the third starter names a specific holding in the real build; it's shown as [종목] here per the privacy call. Designing by turning things off

ChatGPT offers four capabilities. I enabled one.

  • Web search: on. Live prices and news are the entire function. Without it the GPT answers from memory, which for this task is worse than not answering.
  • Canvas: off. She told me chat is what's comfortable, and "zero new things to learn" is the whole principle. A feature that opens her answer in an unfamiliar side panel is friction, not power.
  • Image generation: off. Nothing here needs a generated picture, and leaving it on invites cluttered answers full of charts she didn't ask for.
  • Code interpreter: off. Built for calculation and data analysis. This is news, translation, and price lookup.

Reading an English report she photographs is built in and unaffected by the image toggle: one of her real use cases, kept working without turning anything else on. Most of the design here was subtraction.

07

Outcome

Test 1, v1.1. My mom opened the GPT independently and asked it a trade question. It told her the decision was hers to make. She didn't follow up. That failure drove the entire Decision 02 rebuild, documented as it happened, not smoothed over afterward.

Test 2, v1.2, on a real purchase. She used the rebuilt version independently, on a stock she had just bought. Not a test scenario, but a live decision she was already in the middle of. Instead of walking away, which is what happened in Test 1, she came back with feature requests: a price band for short-term spikes, and whether a short-term dip means hold or take profit. Both were the thing the GPT categorically could not say. Those requests became Decision 03.

Test 3, v1.3, the version that answers them. Her response, over KakaoTalk:

"Showing it like this means I don't have to search one by one. It's like having a briefing secretary. And it's good that I can get a read on the Korean market in advance."이렇게 보여주니까 일일이 찾지 않아도 되고 브리핑해주는 비서처럼 아주 좋아. 글고 한국 시장을 미리 예측 할수 있게 되어 좋아
"Compared to last time, it gives more information on foreign investor flows and program trading, and that makes investment decisions easier."저번보다 좀더 외국인 수급동향과 프로그램 매매등 추정해서 알려주어 투자결정에 좀더 유리해졌어

Two separate things in there. The first is the outcome the whole project was aimed at: she isn't assembling the picture source by source anymore, and she gets the U.S.-market-to-Korean-market read she asked for at first contact.

The second is narrower and matters more to me. What she names as the improvement is the supply-and-demand read, which is precisely the material Decision 03's vocabulary made it possible to hand her. The reframing wasn't a legal workaround that technically satisfied the rule while leaving her where she started. It reached her as the thing she'd asked for.

The change I care about most isn't a feature working, though. It's that she stopped issuing one-way commands like "translate this" and started having back-and-forth conversations with it, following up, pushing back, asking again. She's treating it as her tool now, not something borrowed from her kids' generation.

08

Reflection

What went well. Letting the research kill my original plan. I wanted to build something; the interview said the thing she needed already existed and just wasn't set up for her. Following that took the project from "an app my mom would politely ignore" to something she actually uses. The platform barrier was zero from day one. Her feedback in testing was never "I don't know how to use this," always about what the answer said.

What was hardest. Accepting that the first version failed for the exact reason I'd built it to fix. v1.1's refusal reproduced the "decide for yourself" pattern that had already made her give up on ChatGPT once. It took putting it in front of her to see that a legally correct answer can still be a useless one, and it would have been easy to read her reaction as a wording problem and adjust the tone instead of the structure.

What I'd do differently. Get a rough version in front of her earlier. Every useful signal in this project came from watching her use it, not from refining it beforehand. Three rounds of that produced more than any amount of building in isolation would have. I'd also capture her exact words from the first interview; a few of my earliest research quotes are paraphrased rather than verbatim, and exact wording is what makes research documentation trustworthy.

What's next. Today's Stock was never meant to be the whole thing. It's the first instance of what I think the right structure is: one GPT per task, not one assistant stretched across all of them. The next one is whichever task she's currently working around, set up the same way. The method is the deliverable here, and the method is now tested.

Set up the tool for the task, and the person will use it. She was already proving that at the doctor's office.
